Questions and answers

Plain answers to the questions people ask when they first see this page. Click any question to open it.

Reading the chart

What are the three lines?

Each line is the system's running estimate of how likely a seizure is in the next ten minutes, from 0 at the bottom (no sign of anything) to 1 at the top (as sure as the model gets).

Blue is the opinion of the brain signal alone, taken from the EEG electrodes.

Orange is the opinion of the heart signal alone, taken from the ECG lead.

Green is the joint opinion when both signals are fed into one model. It is the number the system acts on.

What does the green "fused" line actually mean?

It is the verdict of a model that sees the brain features and the heart features at the same time. It is not a simple average of blue and orange. The model learns on this patient's other seizures how much to trust each stream, so when the heart recording is poor quality the green line leans almost entirely on the brain. When both streams rise together the green line rises faster and more confidently than either alone, which is the whole reason for fusing them.

In a sentence a doctor might use. Blue is the EEG opinion, orange is the ECG opinion, green is the joint opinion, and an alarm is the joint opinion staying confident for long enough to be believed.

What are the dashed lines?

They are the alarm thresholds, one per stream. A stream raises an alarm when it stays above its dashed line for three readings in a row, which is about seven and a half seconds at the two and a half second step used here. In this replay each line sits at a fixed 0.99 rather than a fitted value, and even that high setting leaves about three false alarms an hour on this patient. That is far more than any product could ship, and it is what the convergence gate further down the page exists to cut. Nothing here was tuned on the seizure you are watching.

What are the shaded bands?

The orange band is the ten minutes before the seizure where a warning would be useful. It ends two minutes before onset because a warning that arrives in the last two minutes is too late to act on, so those two minutes are left out of scoring. The red band is the seizure itself, as marked by the hospital's neurologists.

A catch means an alarm fired somewhere inside the orange band. An alarm outside it, with no seizure following, is a false alarm.

How far before the seizure does the warning come?

The system is built to look ten minutes ahead. On the four seizures caught here the alarms came between seven and a half and eleven and a half minutes before onset. That is the realistic shape of the warning today. It is enough time to sit down, move away from a road or a stove, tell someone, or let an implant prepare, and not enough time to drive home.

The gate tile can turn on earlier than the alarm, because the gate only needs both streams to be above their lines at the same moment, while an alarm needs a stream to stay there.

Which of the five seizures is the strongest case to show?

Seizure 2 in the drop-down. The brain line, the heart line and the fused line all raise their alarm about eight minutes before onset, and the convergence gate is active from the start of the warning window, so every part of the page lights up in the order it is meant to. Seizure 5 shows the fused line catching what the brain line alone missed. Seizure 3 shows the heart line firing where the brain line never does.

Seizure 1 is a miss, the only one of the five, and it is left in on purpose. A demo that only shows the hits is a sales video, not evidence.

Why did it miss some seizures?

Three honest reasons. First, this patient has only five recorded seizures, so each model is trained on four, which is very little data to learn from. Second, not every seizure announces itself. Some have a slow build-up in the brain and heart over minutes, others switch on with almost no warning, and no monitor can predict the second kind from the signals it has. Third, the thresholds are set deliberately high so that the system does not cry wolf, and a cautious system will let some real ones through. Lowering the lines would catch more and would also alarm more often when nothing is coming.

What you are looking at

Is this one patient or many?

The replay is one patient. She is an adult from the University of Siena's public epilepsy set, recorded in hospital with 29 scalp electrodes and a chest ECG lead over about five hours. She had five seizures across five recording sessions, and the drop-down lets you watch each one. The page shows one person on purpose, because that is the unit the system works on. Every model here is trained on one person's own earlier seizures and then watched on that same person's next seizure, which is also how every implanted stimulator in clinical use is set up.

Behind the page sits a much larger test. The same code was run, one patient at a time, on 32 people at two hospitals, 24 children and young adults at Boston Children's Hospital and 8 adults at Siena with a usable heart lead, about 840 hours of seizure-free recording and 171 lead seizures in all. The table below gives those pooled results. This patient was chosen for the replay because she has both signals, five seizures, and shows the fused model doing what it is built to do.

What code is actually running?

Python, about 3,400 lines in the seizure package and 550 in the consent ledger, plus the scripts that run the experiments and write the tables. Nothing is a black box. The pieces are, in order of the signal's journey, a reader that places every hospital file on one timeline, a feature stage that turns each five-second slice of each electrode into numbers, a heart stage that finds each beat and measures the rhythm, a labeller that decides what counts as "before a seizure" and "far from one", a classifier with its threshold rule, a fusion and gating stage for the two streams, an onset detector, and the ledger that signs and checks consent.

The classifier at the centre is a linear discriminant, which is deliberately plain. With four or five training seizures per person, anything more elaborate memorises noise and looks wonderful until it meets a new seizure. Plainness here is a feature, because every number on this page can be reproduced by anyone with the public data and a laptop, and the whole evaluation of 32 patients runs in under an hour on cached features.

How is the model trained for one patient?

Five steps, repeated for every seizure the patient has.

1. Hold one seizure out. It is not touched until the end.

2. Label the rest. The ten minutes before each remaining seizure are marked "before", anything more than fifteen minutes from any seizure is marked "far", and the seizure itself and its aftermath are left out.

3. Measure. Every two and a half seconds, about 160 numbers from the brain electrodes and 7 from the heart. How much power sits in each rhythm band, how tightly slow and fast rhythms are coupled, how disordered the signal is, how the heart's beat-to-beat interval is drifting, and how each of those is trending over the last two and five minutes.

4. Learn and set the alarm line. The classifier learns which small shifts in those numbers separate "before" from "far". The alarm threshold is then chosen on data the classifier did not train on, at the lowest level that keeps false alarms under a target rate. That choice is never made on the held-out seizure.

5. Replay the held-out seizure as if live and score it. Did an alarm fire inside the warning window, how early, and how many alarms fired when nothing was coming.

Then the next seizure is held out and the whole thing starts again. The lines on this page are the replays from step 5, which is why they are honest. The detector that spots a seizure once it has begun is trained the same way, with the labels swapped to "during" and "not during".

Could a doctor watching the screen just see it coming?

No, and this is the finding that makes the field interesting. Once a seizure has started it is obvious on an EEG, which is why detection is the easier half and why hospital monitoring units employ people to watch for it. The minutes before are different. To the eye, and to the trained eye, the trace in the run-up looks like ordinary background. There are no spikes to point at.

The research record is clear on this. A long review in the journal Brain in 2007 went back through decades of claims that the preictal state could be seen or measured, found that most had not survived proper testing, and set out the rules now used here, holding seizures out and comparing against a random alarm. The first implanted prediction device, trialled in Australia in 2013 on fifteen patients, showed that a preictal state does exist and is measurable in some people, with warnings that worked well for some and poorly for others. Public competitions since then on recordings like these have reached about the same conclusion. The signal is real, it is small, it varies from person to person, and it is found by measuring many quantities together over minutes, not by looking.

That is why the page shows three computed lines rather than the raw trace. What a human would see on the raw trace in that window is nothing at all.

Why is it easy to miss a seizure, and what happens when one is missed?

Four reasons, all visible on this page. Some seizures announce themselves over minutes and some switch on with almost no run-up, and no monitor can forecast the second kind from the signals it has. Each person's model learns from only a handful of their own seizures, so it has seen very few examples of what "before" looks like for them. The alarm line is set high on purpose so the system does not cry wolf, and a cautious system lets some real ones through. And the scoring here is strict, so an alarm that fires in the last two minutes before onset is counted as a miss for the forecast, even though a device could still act on it.

A miss is not a disaster, because of what is layered underneath. The onset detector catches the seizure within seconds once it begins, which is all an implanted stimulator needs, and the consent gate sits in front of both so nothing acts without the patient's standing permission. The forecast is the bonus layer that buys minutes when it fires. It is reported with its misses because a system that will act on a brain has to be built by people who write down what did not work.

The safety gate and the consent ledger

What is the convergence gate?

It is a second check before anything is allowed to act on the brain. The gate is only active when the brain stream and the heart stream are both above their lines at the same time. Two independent witnesses have to agree. Requiring agreement cut false alarms by about 44 per cent on the eight Siena subjects and by about a third on the one Boston patient who also carried a heart lead, which is the only Boston case with the second signal. The price is sensitivity, since a seizure that only one stream sees is no longer acted on, so the gate belongs in front of a device that acts on the brain rather than in front of a warning sent to a carer. If the heart lead is missing or the signal is too noisy to trust, the gate falls back to a stricter brain-only test rather than silently waving things through.

What is the intervention tile?

It stands in for whatever device would act on the warning, for example an implanted stimulator, a wearable that vibrates and calls a carer, or simply an app alert. On this page it only changes to "authorised trigger" when three things are true at once. The fused alarm has fired, the gate agrees, and the consent ledger says access is permitted. Take any one away and it stays idle.

What is the consent ledger?

It is the patient's own permission, written as a chain of signed records rather than a tick box. When a person enrols, two cryptographic keys are created on their own device. One signs consent, naming what the system may do, to which signals, by which device and for how long. The other exists only to say no. Every record is linked by hash to the record before it, so changing a single character anywhere breaks the chain, and a broken chain is treated as a reason to refuse everyone until a human looks.

The device checks the chain before every single action. There is no administrator override and no manufacturer's master key. Building one in would break the chain.

What happens when I press "Revoke consent"?

A signed withdrawal record is appended to the chain and the intervention tile goes to refused on the next check, whatever the prediction lines are doing. The latency shown under the tile was measured for real. A revocation was written to the ledger and the authorisation check was polled until it refused. Over 1,800 trials across nine configurations the median ran from 21 to 167 milliseconds, depending on how many records the ledger held and how often the device polled, with 74 milliseconds in the middle case. The worst single trial was under a third of a second. "Re-grant" writes a fresh consent record and access resumes. Withdrawing is meant to be the easiest thing the person ever does. Giving authority back is the step that deserves friction.

Who can revoke, and how do they do it in real life?

The patient, always, with a tap on a phone or a press-and-hold on a physical control on the wearable. A carer or clinician can hold a second key that can stop the system and can never start it. That asymmetry is the heart of the design. For a person who cannot move or speak and whose only channel to the world is the interface itself, withdrawal lives inside the same yes-and-no conversation they use for everything else, as a reserved sequence that is confirmed twice so that a stray thought cannot switch it off and a settled one can.

Pulling the plug is not withdrawal. It is absence. If the device cannot reach a chain it can verify, it does nothing. It fails closed, not open, and every consent record carries its own expiry.

Devices and signals

What is the patient actually wearing?

In this recording, hospital equipment. Scalp EEG electrodes glued to the head and a standard ECG lead on the chest, which is the gold standard and the reason this is a replay rather than a live feed.

In everyday life the brain signal comes from a wearable EEG. That is the natural fit for this system, because the models here were built on scalp EEG and a wearable reads from the same place. The options already on the market or in trials are a headband or cap with dry electrodes, an in-ear EEG earpiece that sits like an earbud, a behind-the-ear patch, and glasses or a headset with electrodes in the frame. They give fewer channels than the hospital montage and a noisier signal, so the first job before a product was to re-run these experiments with only the channels a wearable can reach. That has now been done. On a seven-channel wearable montage the onset detector catches 108 of 181 seizures at about one false alarm every three and a half hours, against 158 of 181 on the full hospital montage at about one every two and a quarter hours. Those are different alarm rates, so that detection comparison is not like for like, and the full montage figure flatters itself by spending half again as many alarms. Both are being re-measured, because the same calibration fault applies to the detector and there it is worse. Asked for a budget of 0.05 alarms per hour the detector delivered 0.399, and asked for 0.20 it delivered 0.440, so below about 0.4 the rate hardly responded to what was requested. On the full montage that makes the published detection curve a single operating point labelled five different ways. The wearable montage does respond across that range and its curve is real. The forecast arm was reported at 27 of 139, though that arm had been scored at a stricter horizon than the full montage. Scored at identical settings, which had never been done until now, the wearable montage and the full hospital montage return exactly the same forecast, 8 of 59 lead seizures each, at alarm rates within six thousandths of one another. A wearable therefore keeps most of the detection and, on this evidence, gives up nothing measurable in the forecast. For people who need the signal all day for years, a sub-scalp electrode under the skin behind the ear or a small implant gives a cleaner, more stable recording, which is why those exist as the next step up rather than the starting point.

The heart signal comes from a chest patch that records a true ECG for days, or, with some loss of precision, a watch or ring that measures pulse from the skin. Watches and rings read the pulse rather than the heart's electrical trace, so they are a good proxy for heart rate and rhythm trends but not identical to what you see here.

Is it only checking the heart and the brain?

In this demo, yes, because those are the two signals present in the public hospital recordings and the only two that have been tested. The architecture is not limited to two. Any sensor that produces a stream of numbers over time can be added as another witness, with its own model and its own line on the chart. The candidates that make clinical sense are skin conductance, which is a sweat response already used by some seizure-detection wristbands, movement from an accelerometer, breathing rate, blood oxygen, and skin temperature. A camera could be a witness as well, watching for posture change or repetitive movement, though it raises its own privacy questions and the consent ledger would have to name it explicitly. None of those have been evaluated yet and this page does not claim they would help.

Would the system need a live person watching?

No. Everything on this page is computed by the software from the signals alone, with no human in the loop. A clinician would set the policy, for example what the device may do and how cautious the thresholds should be, and the patient would hold the consent. The point of the design is that the device can act, or refuse to act, in milliseconds without anyone watching a screen.

Why the heart at all? Is the brain not enough?

Seizures that begin in the temporal lobe often disturb the part of the nervous system that paces the heart, so heart rate and its beat-to-beat variability start to shift in the minutes before onset. The brain signal is usually the stronger witness, but on the Italian cohort used here the model that saw both signals predicted 12 of 32 seizures at a ten-minute horizon, against 8 of 32 for the brain alone. A heart lead is cheap, comfortable, and far easier to wear all day than scalp electrodes, which is why it is worth having as the second witness.

How honest is this?

Is this real data or a simulation?

Real. The recording is from a public clinical dataset released by the University of Siena, with seizure times marked by their neurologists. The lines were produced by the neurocore pipeline from that recording and have not been edited. The consent panel drives a real ledger using real signatures. Nothing here is drawn by hand.

Was the model allowed to see this seizure during training?

No. For each seizure in the drop-down, the models and the thresholds were built using only the patient's other seizures and then run on this one as if it were happening live. This is called leave-one-seizure-out evaluation. It is slower and gives lower numbers than the shortcuts that inflate many published results, and it is the only kind of number that survives contact with a new patient.

How good is it overall?

Not yet good enough for a clinic, and not pretending to be. On the largest public benchmark the brain-only models catch 36 of 139 lead seizures, which is 26 per cent, at about one false alarm every seven and a half hours. An earlier version of this page gave 42 per cent. That figure came from a calibration fault which let the alarm threshold spend more alarms than the budget it stated, and it is withdrawn. Under stricter labelling, which also requires five minutes of warning and does not score a seizure that follows another within four hours, the same models catch 8 of 59 and the result no longer separates from chance. Scored brain-only under those same rules, the Siena cohort catches 4 of 22 and is indistinguishable from chance, so it confirms nothing. The fused Siena figures below were measured under shorter settings and have not been re-run at matched rules. Requiring the two streams to agree cuts false alarms by around half on every dataset tried, at a cost in seizures caught. The consent and revocation layer, by contrast, is finished in the sense that matters. It runs, it refuses, and it has measured numbers.

What would make the prediction better?

More seizures per patient, so each model has more to learn from. Longer recordings, so the models can learn what normal looks like across a whole day. Confirmation on an independent dataset. And deeper models trained on a proper machine rather than a laptop. The thresholds can also be tuned per patient and per use, since an implant that acts on the brain should be far more cautious than an app that sends a message to a carer.

Where could this go?

Seizures are the starting point because the need is urgent and the ground truth is unambiguous. The consent layer does not care whose electrodes sit at the other end of it. Any closed loop that reads a brain and acts on it, whether for epilepsy, panic, tremor, fatigue or speech, has the same second question to answer. Is the system permitted to act on this person right now, and can it prove it? This page is a working answer to that question, running on real data, with the off switch in the patient's hand.

The harder questions

Why a ten-minute window and not an hour?

Because the Siena recordings are short, mostly under an hour per file, so a thirty-minute window would leave almost no "far from a seizure" time to learn from. On the Boston set, where the recordings run for days, the window is thirty minutes and the mean catch per patient arrives anywhere from one minute to twenty-seven minutes ahead, with a median across patients of sixteen. The window is a scoring choice, not a limit of the method. A longer window catches more and is vaguer about when. A shorter window is sharper and misses more. A product would let the clinician set it per patient and per use.

What does a false alarm cost, and why is one an hour acceptable here?

It depends entirely on what the alarm does. If it sends a quiet message to a phone, one an hour is a nuisance and a patient will switch it off within a week. If it tells an implant to deliver a pulse that the patient cannot feel, as the responsive stimulators already in use do, one an hour is routine, those devices fire hundreds of times a day. The thresholds here are tuned for the cautious end, and the convergence gate exists so that the brain-acting path can be held to a far stricter standard than the warning path, inside the same system, from the same signals.

How is this different from the implants already on the market?

Today's responsive stimulator senses the seizure after it has started and fires within milliseconds. It does not forecast, it uses one signal, and its permission model is a clinician's programming session. This system adds three things in front of that. A forecast minutes ahead from the same brain signal. A second, independent witness from the heart, with a rule that the two must agree before the brain may be touched. And a consent record signed by the patient, checked before every action, that the patient alone can withdraw, with the withdrawal measured in milliseconds rather than promised in a policy. The existing implant could sit at the end of this chain unchanged.

What do the four patent filings cover?

Four provisional applications were filed with the United States Patent Office in July 2026. In plain terms they cover, first, the consent ledger itself, a hash-chained record of signed permissions and revocations that a device must verify before every access, with the fail-closed behaviour shown here. Second, prediction from more than one physiological stream with a convergence gate that requires independent agreement before an intervention is authorised. Third, authenticated control of the device, so that only a trigger carrying valid consent and valid agreement can reach the hardware. Fourth, consent and control expressed through the brain interface itself, for a person whose only channel to the world is that interface. This page exercises the first three directly.

What does "p = 0.002" mean on the Siena result?

It is the chance that a system with no information at all, raising alarms at random at the same rate as this one, would have caught 12 or more of the 32 seizures by luck. Two in a thousand. The comparison is deliberately unkind to the model, it uses the random predictor from the 2006 paper that the field adopted after the early false claims were cleared out, and it is computed at the model's own false-alarm rate so a system cannot look good by alarming constantly. The brain-only result on the same patients has p of 0.046, meaningful but closer to the line, which is why the heart matters.

Why hold out the seizure being tested, and what goes wrong if you do not?

Because a model that has seen a seizure's own run-up during training will recognise it on the test, and the score will be meaningless. This is the leakage that inflated many published results, including some that reported near-perfect prediction and then failed on new data. Here every seizure is scored by a model that never saw it, and the alarm threshold was set on data that will not be scored either. The numbers are lower because of this, and they are the only kind that will survive a prospective trial.

What about the patient's privacy?

The raw signals never need to leave the device. The ledger carries no neural data at all, only signatures, hashes, timestamps and scope, so a hospital or an employer can audit that consent existed without seeing a single sample of brain activity. Access scope is part of the signed record, so a system permitted to watch for seizures is not permitted to do anything else, and a request outside scope is refused like an expired one. The public recordings used on this page are anonymised releases from the two hospitals, used under their own licence.

What happens if the device loses contact with the ledger?

It stops. A device that cannot reach a chain it can verify does nothing, which is the opposite of how most connected hardware behaves. Every consent record also carries its own expiry, so a person who becomes unable to act is protected by silence rather than by whoever last touched the settings. Disconnection is treated as absence of permission, not as permission by default.

What is the next twelve months?

Two of the four things this page used to list here are done. The whole evaluation has been re-run on only the electrodes a wearable can carry, and the onset detector now gives every seizure a timed catch even when the forecast misses. Both results are in the table above and in the answers below.

Four things remain, in order. Confirm the brain-plus-heart result on a third, independent set of recordings it has never seen, which is the single thing this work most needs and the thing its absence is most open to attack on. Re-run the wearable forecast at the same prediction horizon as the full montage, because the two were scored at different horizons and the wearable number is probably pessimistic as a result. State the per-patient fitting requirement plainly wherever the system is described, since a model trained on everyone else reaches 14 per cent against 60 per cent at the same alarm rate, so the device has to be fitted to its wearer and cannot be shipped pre-trained. And bring in a university partner with a monitoring unit so the system can run live on a ward rather than on replays, which is the step that turns a demonstration into evidence.
